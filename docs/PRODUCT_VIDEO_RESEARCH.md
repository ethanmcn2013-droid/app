# Product videos — field research

**Status: research. Commissioned by the operator 2026-07-31.**
**Series:** design pages (`DESIGN_PAGES_RESEARCH.md`) · product videos (this
file) · product launches (`PRODUCT_LAUNCH_RESEARCH.md`).

The brief: find the best product explainer and launch videos ever made, work
out why they work, and ideate what Signal Studio films should be.

**Method.** Four parallel research passes (the historical canon · the modern
SaaS craft wing · hardware and AI-era films including failures · the studios,
tools, and craft knowledge), verified via search 2026-07-31. Same egress-proxy
caveat as the design-pages research: statuses are search-verified, not
live-watched. Hard sourcing rule applied throughout: **every watch link below
appeared literally in a search result; where none did, the entry gives an
exact search string instead.** Claims resting on memory are marked
*(unverified)*.

---

## 1. The genre

A product video succeeds when someone who has never seen the product finishes
it, understands the product, and feels something about the company. The genre
test, after Sandwich founder Adam Lisagor's method: state the problem, show
the solution, explain it, restate it — with **no barrier to understanding**.

The core tension of the form, sharpened by the AI era: production value used
to signal seriousness; now **verifiability** does. Audiences forensically
scan launch footage by default, and the counter-demo — a journalist repeating
your claim with bare hands — is a permanent genre.

## 2. The canon, ranked

Ranked for feel first, then craft, then influence. Scores 1–10 from the field
passes.

### 1 · Dollar Shave Club — "Our Blades Are F***ing Great" (2012)

**Feel 10 · Craft 9.** The consensus greatest launch video ever. CEO Michael
Dubin walks deadpan through his own warehouse in one escalating tracking
shot — machete, toddler shaving a man's head, bear costume — delivering
direct-response copy as absurdist stand-up. Cost $4,500 (directed by Lucia
Aniello, later of *Hacks*). Site crashed in the first hour; 12,000 orders in
48 hours; company sold to Unilever for $1B in 2016.
Watch: https://vimeo.com/92288886

### 2 · Apple — the keynote-as-film and the design liturgy

**Feel 10 (2007) · Craft 10.** Three landmarks: the iPhone introduction
(Macworld 2007) — the "three revolutionary products" fake-out, still taught
as the best product presentation ever (watch: search "Steve Jobs iPhone
introduction 2007"); the Jony Ive white-void films whose "raw aluminium of
exceptional purity" narration set the grammar for a decade of hardware films
(unibody film: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lJx6cF-H__I); and the modern
counter-lesson — "Crush!" (2024), a hydraulic press pulverizing instruments
into an iPad, read as Big Tech crushing culture in the AI mood; Apple issued
a rare public apology and pulled it from TV. The same metaphor Apple used for
decades, inverted by a change in the room's mood.

### 3 · Sandwich — "So Yeah, We Tried Slack" (2014)

**Feel 9 · Craft 9.** The SaaS canon film. Opens on the true story of
Lisagor *declining* Stewart Butterfield's request ("products like that never
work") — the ad's premise is the vendor's own skepticism, and the demo is the
studio's real workflow converting on screen. 1.3M+ views; in 2014 "a Sandwich
video" was the status symbol of a Valley launch.
Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B6zVzWU95Sw

### 4 · Old Spice — "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" (2010)

**Feel 10 · Craft 10.** Formative for launch voice even though it's adjacent
to the explainer genre: the seamless in-camera scene morphs, then the real
innovation — the Response machine. Over ~2.5 days Wieden+Kennedy scripted,
shot, and posted **186 personalized video replies** to individual commenters,
some produced in 10–15 minutes. Twitter followers +2,700%; body-wash sales
doubled by July. The reply became part of the film.
Watch: search "Old Spice The Man Your Man Could Smell Like"

### 5 · Teenage Engineering — the deadpan hardware film

**Feel 9 · Craft 10.** Locked-off camera, products dead-flat on seamless
white, only hands ever appear, spec captions in their own typeface, and the
music is made on the device being sold *(register details part-unverified)*.
No adjectives, no lifestyle B-roll: the runtime is the machine being
operated. A $1,499 tape recorder sells on this register alone.
Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=88Apt7lhY64 (EP-133 performance demo)

### 6 · Google Japan — the Gboard gag-hardware films

**Feel 10 · Craft 9.** Annual joke keyboards presented in full deadpan
corporate-explainer format — diagrams, engineer interviews, spec claims —
for deliberately absurd hardware that actually works and ships as open
source with build guides (the 1,650mm single-row keyboard; the Möbius-strip
keyboard). TechCrunch: "might be the best running joke in tech." The
reference standard for committing to the bit.
Watch: search "Google Japan Gboard bar version"

### 7 · Harmon Brothers — Squatty Potty / Purple (2013–)

**Feel 8 · Craft 8.** The industrialized comedic-conversion formula: an
absurd spokes-character (prince + soft-serve unicorn; Goldilocks with a
raw-egg rig), a taboo problem stated bluntly, a legitimate science demo
mid-comedy, repeated hard CTAs, then heavy paid seeding. Squatty Potty: 170M
social views, online sales +600%. Purple: $0 → $100M run rate in 14 months.
Still active (2025: ~50 staff, $500k minimum).
Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4BvwpjaGZCQ (Purple raw-egg test)

### 8 · Linear — the modern SaaS grammar

**Feel 7 · Craft 10.** 45–90 second dense feature reels: dark-mode real UI,
minimal or no narration, one job per video, motion blur and micro-bounce as
UI snaps into place, huge single-word typography read in sequence. In-house.
The most imitated register in B2B motion — and by 2025 the launch video
dissolves into the landing page itself (the homepage hero is an autonomously
animating live product demo).
Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6dIwFoQ0eVg (Introducing Linear
Releases) · release stream: linear.app/now

### 9 · Notion — launching with characters instead of UI

**Feel 9 · Craft 9.** The one big SaaS that launches with drawn characters:
Roman Muradov's New-Yorker-cartoon world, and Notion AI embodied as "Nosy,"
a face-character whose eyebrows wave while it thinks and whose face falls
apart on errors — animated by BUCK. Character gives memory something to hold
that a feature list can't.
Watch: buck.co/work/notion-ai

### 10 · mmhmm — the demo that demonstrates itself (2020)

**Feel 9 · Craft 7.** Phil Libin performs the software while explaining it —
his shrunken body pointing at slides, backgrounds cycling — so every claim is
proven by the medium in real time. Raised $31M pre-launch largely off the
demo's virality. The purest software version of "the page is the argument."
Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tHytsc0FIoI ("Welcome to mmhmm")

**Near-canon, one line each:** Dropbox's 2009 MVP screencast (a demo of
vaporware salted with Hacker News in-jokes; waitlist 5,000 → 75,000 in a
day — influence 10, pixels 5) · Google "Parisian Love" (2010 — a love story
told entirely in a search box; UI as narrative, made by five students) ·
Common Craft "RSS in Plain English" (2007 — the paper-cutout origin of the
whole genre) · Blendtec "Will It Blend?" (2006 — one spec turned into an
infinitely repeatable game show) · GoPro (the customer's footage is the ad;
craft outsourced to reality) · Fireship "in 100 seconds" (the default
grammar of dev explainers: ~100 deadpan seconds, one dry joke per ten) ·
Playdate Update (Nintendo-Direct format performed by actual staff with
self-deprecating bits) · Nothing (founder-to-camera community updates as the
primary launch medium) · Ramp (actual TV craft: The Office's
cinematographer directing Kevin-from-The-Office in their 2026 Super Bowl
spot) · Figma Config openers (Copenhagen studio Relay + Figma Brand Studio;
the industry's annual motion flex) · Raycast (the cleanest 2026 launch arc:
a "Coming 2026" countdown teaser paying off in a two-word title, "It's
out") · PostHog ("if any other SaaS company could have written it, we don't
run it") · Dyson ("engineering, explained" — failed prototypes as the ad) ·
DJI (every hero frame shot on the product being sold) · Descript (quarterly
"Seasons" as live-streamed shows) · Cursor 2.0 (agent-coding demos as the
new hero footage).

## 3. The cautionary tier

The failures teach more than the wins here, because the failure mode is
always the same: **the video wrote a check the product couldn't cash.**

- **Google Gemini (Dec 2023)** — the canonical case. The "hands-on" video
  implied real-time voice-and-video interaction; it was stills plus text
  prompts, latency edited out. Google admitted staging. The correction is
  the real lesson: at I/O 2024, Project Astra was shown as a **single-take
  live demo** — verifiable liveness became the credibility claim itself.
- **Humane Ai Pin (2023)** — the launch film contained its own errors (wrong
  eclipse location; almond protein off by ~4x), uncorrected. The errors
  became the launch. Sold to HP for parts within 18 months.
- **Rabbit R1 (2024)** — the keynote camera cut away at the actual booking
  moment; the named "Large Action Model" was later alleged to be hardcoded
  scripts. Naming a fake architecture invites forensic debunking.
- **Juicero (2017)** — Bloomberg squeezed the juice packs by hand on video,
  faster than the $400 machine. The counter-demo era began here: your claims
  must survive a journalist's bare hands.
- **Apple Siri ad (2024–25)** — advertised a personal-context Siri that then
  slipped indefinitely; ad pulled, false-advertising suit followed. Shipping
  the ad before the feature is now legal exposure, even for the most
  disciplined launcher alive.
- **OpenAI GPT-5 (Aug 2025)** — the "chart crime": launch graphics whose bar
  heights contradicted their own labels, apologized for by the CEO. Live
  demos buy authenticity; sloppy supporting graphics spend it.
- **YC × Optifye (Feb 2025)** — a demo role-playing a factory boss
  surveilling which workers slack; deleted after backlash. A demo dramatizes
  your values, not just your product.

## 4. What separates the top one percent

1. **The demo must be true.** Single-take, real speed, verifiable — liveness
   is the new production value (Astra's correction; the counter-demo era).
   Never show a capability that doesn't exist yet.
2. **Founder on camera beats anonymous gloss.** Dubin, Libin, Pei, Miller,
   Sasser, Lisagor's whole thesis; the 2025–26 practitioner consensus is the
   founder-led demo. Conversational beats overproduced.
3. **Skepticism is the best opener.** Sandwich's premise is declining the
   job; DSC opens by mocking its own category. Starting from doubt buys the
   right to be believed.
4. **One idea, fully compressed.** One tracking shot; two-word payoffs; 100
   seconds. Wistia's data: engagement holds ~50% through minute three, then
   falls — front-load, and 90–120s is the canonical length.
5. **Real UI at real speed is winning.** The 2026 consensus across roundups:
   real product footage over stylized concept film. The Linear/Screen Studio
   grammar has fully mainstreamed; the launch video is dissolving into the
   landing page.
6. **Deadpan compounds; hype decays.** Teenage Engineering, Google Japan,
   Fireship, Playdate. Dry delivery makes big claims feel like understatement
   and survives re-watching.
7. **Character gives memory a handle.** Nosy's collapsing face, hedgehogs,
   the unicorn. People forget features and remember faces.
8. **The response is part of the film.** Old Spice's 186 replies, Nothing
   answering critics on camera, Playdate acknowledging delays head-on. Plan
   the reply mechanism, not just the drop.
9. **Sound is proof, not garnish.** TE scores films on the instrument being
   sold; the music is the spec sheet.
10. **Values leak through demos.** Crush! and Optifye died on values, not
    craft. Anthropic's "Keep thinking" campaign argues values while OpenAI
    performs capability live — both work; choose deliberately.

## 5. The arc

Cheap authenticity beats budgets (2006–2012: Blendtec's $50 first video,
Common Craft's whiteboard, DSC's $4,500) → the Sandwich era makes dry
founder storytelling the Valley default and Harmon industrializes comedic
conversion (2012–2018) → compression and grammar-formation (2019–2023:
Fireship's 100 seconds, Linear's reels, Screen Studio democratizing the
polished screencast) → the credibility era (2023–2026): staged AI demos get
caught and punished, liveness and founder-cam become trust technology,
studios move in-house (Duolingo bought its animation studio outright), and
real product UI wins the format debate. Production value inflated away;
verifiability and voice are the scarce assets. Both point the same
direction as the design-pages research: **handmade, provable, in-register.**

## 6. Ideation — Signal Studio films

Grounding: the dry voice (the banned-word list is practically a deadpan
format spec), the refusal list, T·-numbered cycles with CHANGELOG dispatch
entries, four modules, the indigo dot, no-celebration register. The voice is
genuinely suited to the two strongest registers in the genre: the deadpan
hardware film and the honest founder walkthrough.

**A. "90 seconds, no adjectives."** The house format contract: every feature
film ≤90s, real UI at real speed, captions instead of voiceover, zero
adjectives (the brand's banned-word list enforced as a film rule), one dry
line at the end. Teenage Engineering's register applied to software.

**B. The dispatch film.** One per shipped cycle, titled by its T·number,
published with the CHANGELOG entry: founder on camera for 15 seconds, then
screen capture at typing speed for 90. The cycle numbering is already a
brand asset — let it accumulate like Playdate Updates. This also builds the
response habit: when something breaks or a critic lands a point, the next
dispatch answers it on camera.

**C. The refusal ad.** The video version of `/principles`: the eight
refusals read to camera, deadpan, while the product quietly does its actual
job in the background. Sandwich's skepticism-as-premise, DSC's
category-mockery, in our register. Best shipped once an audience exists to
get the joke.

**D. The dot idents.** Four 10–15 second films where the indigo dot is the
only character: it rolls into a checkbox and settles (Tasks), tunes static
into a clean line (Signal), traces a timeline (Timeline), lands at the end
of a sentence (Notes). A motion identity built from the one ornament the
brand already owns — the character lesson without adopting a mascot. Build
in Rive so the same rigs run on the site (ties to design-page concept B).

**E. The no-cuts badge.** A standing rule, stated on camera once: demos are
single-take at real speed, timestamped, no edits inside a demo. In the
counter-demo era, this is a differentiator that costs nothing because the
product is real. Receipts culture, filmed.

**F. Module in 100 seconds.** Four Fireship-compressed films, one per
module, for the marketing pages' hero slots — the format the audience
already reads as "developer-honest."

**Tooling.** Screen Studio for capture (its grammar is the accepted
standard); Rive for the dot rigs; no AI-generated footage of the product or
crowds — forensic scrutiny is the era, and one caught frame spends years of
receipts. If outsourcing ever makes sense: Sandwich still exists (and takes
equity), Giant Ant / Oddfellows / Ordinary Folk for animation, Buck at
scale, Vidico for volume on a budget.

## 7. Iteration path

- **v1 — the habit.** Ship one dispatch film (B) with the next shipped
  cycle, under the format contract (A). Bar: would a developer quote a line
  of it. Cost: a microphone, Screen Studio, an afternoon.
- **v2 — the grammar.** The four 100-second module films (F) + the first two
  dot idents (D). Adopt the no-cuts badge (E) publicly.
- **v3 — the flex.** The refusal ad (C), once there's an audience; consider
  a Config-style annual moment only when there's a year of dispatches behind
  it.
- **Measure:** watch-through past the first 30 seconds (front-load), quoted
  lines and shares, replies answered on camera, and whether the T·number
  films become expected. No view-count worship — DSC's 12,000 orders beat
  its view count as the metric that mattered.

# Product launches — field research

**Status: research. Commissioned by the operator 2026-07-31.**
**Series:** design pages (`DESIGN_PAGES_RESEARCH.md`) · product videos
(`PRODUCT_VIDEO_RESEARCH.md`) · product launches (this file).

The brief: the launch as a designed moment — announcement, mechanics,
spectacle, aftermath. What the best launches ever actually did, what the
failures actually cost, and what launching should mean for Signal Studio.

**Method.** Four parallel research passes (the all-time canon · software and
AI launch mechanics 2020–2026 · consumer/hardware/campaign launches
2023–2026 · the failure catalog and craft knowledge), search-verified
2026-07-31 with the same egress-proxy caveat as the rest of the series.
Numbers below appeared in cited coverage; memory-only claims were marked
*(unverified)* in the field notes and the weakest are listed at the end.

---

## 1. The genre

A launch is a promissory note with a date attached. Everything in this file
sorts by how the note was written (mechanic), how it was collected
(numbers), and whether it was honored (aftermath). The craft consensus —
YC, Lenny's Newsletter, April Dunford's positioning work — is blunt:
**a launch is a process, not an event**, you launch again and again, and
positioning is the variable that decides whether the same product reads
"so?" or "wow." The strongest empirical pattern across ~40 cases: the
loudest launches often fizzled; the quiet ones compounded.

## 2. The canon, ranked

Ranked for theatre (feel) and business result (effect) together, with the
mechanic named — because the mechanic is the reusable part.

### 1 · iPhone (2007) — the keynote as genre-founder

"Three revolutionary products" repeated until the room realizes it's one
device; announced six months before availability so desire compounded into
retail lines. 270,000 units in two days; a million in 74 days. The lesson
everyone copied: announce theatrically, ship later — and the lesson most
copiers miss: the demo was real.

### 2 · ChatGPT (Nov 2022) — the anti-launch

A free "research preview" shipped via blog post; no keynote, no spend.
1M users in 5 days, ~100M in about 60 — the fastest consumer ramp ever
recorded at the time. Framing it as a throwaway demo removed expectations;
zero friction made every user's screenshot the ad. When the artifact is
genuinely novel, understatement beats staging.

### 3 · Nintendo Switch 2 (Jun 2025) — scarcity as reward

The Direct format (an owned, schedulable announcement channel) plus a
loyalty-gated invite-to-buy system: priority required a year of paid
membership and 50+ played hours — rationing that reads as fan service, not
scalper tax. 2.2M lottery applications in Japan; 3.5M+ units in four days,
the fastest-selling Nintendo hardware ever. Contrast: PS5's 2020 launch,
where 99.2% of first-hour purchase attempts were reseller bots and scalpers
captured the premium.

### 4 · Tesla Model 3 (2016) — the deposit prices belief

$1,000 refundable deposits from unveil night, ~18 months before production:
belief turned into a countable, financeable number. 115K reservations the
first night, 325K in a week (~$14B implied). The companion case is
Cybertruck 2019: the armor-glass demo shattered on stage, the stock fell
6.1% — and $100 deposits still hit 250K within days. A refundable deposit
even makes a botched demo quantifiably survivable.

### 5 · Gmail (Apr 1, 2004) — scarcity on a free product

1GB (100x competitors) launched on April Fools' so the claim itself was the
story, then invite-only for almost three years; invites traded for
$100–$250. Constraining supply of a free product converts users into
recruiters. The essential fine print (see Google Wave below): it only
worked because an invited user could still email *anyone* — solo value
survived the scarcity.

### 6 · Supabase Launch Week — serialization

One feature per weekday, twice a year, with hackathons converting
spectators into contributors — one press cycle turned into five, a calendar
that trains the audience to show up. It became the industry's default: 126
launch weeks by 94 companies in 2024, tracked on launchweek.dev — which
Supabase itself open-sourced, making them the category's reference point.
The originator effect is real, though: the 95th copycat gets a fraction of
the format's power.

### 7 · DeepSeek R1 (Jan 2025) — counter-programming

Open weights + paper + cheap API, dropped quietly the day before a rival's
$500B infrastructure spectacle. The contrast was the story: #1 on the US
App Store within a week; Nvidia fell 17% (~$600B) in a day. For a
credibility-based audience, an artifact outperforms any staged event —
and timing substance against someone else's hype multiplies coverage for
free. Same family: Mistral launching a frontier model as a bare torrent
magnet link on X (2023), and Anthropic's MCP — launched as an open
standard with no keynote, won when every rival adopted it.

### 8 · Windows 95 (Aug 1995) — saturation with one gesture

~$300M campaign, the Rolling Stones licensed, the Empire State Building
lit, The Times' entire print run bought and given away — all anchored to
one teachable gesture: the Start button. 7M copies in five weeks. The
pre-internet proof that a launch needs a single learnable thing at its
center, whatever the budget around it.

### 9 · Duolingo "Death of Duo" (Feb 2025) — the mascot as launch asset

The owl declared dead across every channel, revived only when the community
earned 50 billion XP — which they did (≈5B lessons). 1.7B impressions in
two weeks; ~450 news articles. The rare stunt that converted spectacle
into product usage by gating the resolution on a product metric.

### 10 · GTA VI (2023–26) — scarcity of communication

Near-total silence punctuated by rare, dated artifacts: trailer one set the
24-hour record (90.4M views), trailer two beat the biggest movie trailers
(475M across platforms in 24h); two delays absorbed with short apologetic
statements and intact anticipation. When every utterance is an event, you
control the calendar. Requires artifacts that are undeniable.

**Near-canon, one line each:** Dropbox's referral loop (the strongest
launch is a loop, not an event: 100K → 4M users in 15 months, 2.8x paid
channels) · Threads (a borrowed graph buys 100M installs in 5 days — and a
20% DAU drop the next week; retention is the scoreboard) · Halo 3 (sell
the date as a record attempt: $170M day one, pre-sold) · CeraVe × Michael
Cera (seed a mystery the internet wants to solve; let the debunking be
part of the media plan — 9 Cannes Lions) · Red Bull Stratos (a stunt works
when the brand is the protagonist, not the sponsor) · Lovable (killed a
working brand to relaunch; #1 on Product Hunt and Hacker News the same
day; ~$100M ARR in ~8 months) · Cursor (the counter-case: no launches at
all, fastest SaaS to $100M ARR — a product with daily pull in a talkative
graph makes every first session the launch) · Linear (a year of invite-only
polish as the quality signal itself) · Superhuman (onboarding-as-launch:
mandatory 30-minute sessions; extreme activation, capped growth — the
exclusive brand outlived the exclusivity and became the acquirer's name) ·
Hey.com (pick a public fight your audience already wants fought) ·
Playdate (honest preorder waves + games shipped as seasons; 20K units in
20 minutes, loyalty for years) · Nothing (StockX auctions, Art Basel
reveals, community editions — scarcity + transparency instead of ad
budget) · Meta Ray-Ban Display (both stage demos failed; sold out in 48
hours anyway because the in-store demo delivered — live-demo risk is
survivable when the product demos well in person) · Figma Config (the
conference clock forces portfolio-scale shipping, at the cost of
half-ready betas) · Notion AI (ship the waitlist the moment the wave
breaks: 2M signups in 10 weeks, then the queue as a beta panel) · Labubu /
Pop Mart (drop ritual at global scale: +185% revenue, then −60% stock —
build the pipeline, not the peak).

## 3. The failure catalog

Every failure is one of four notes coming due.

**The product wasn't there.** Quibi ($1.75B raised, dead in six months —
launch capital just amplifies product truth faster) · Cyberpunk 2077
(shipped to hit the marketed date on hardware it didn't run on; delisted
from the PlayStation Store; redemption took three years and an
expansion-scale investment) · No Man's Sky (the founder promised features
that didn't exist; the only proven apology was a decade of free updates) ·
Humane and Rabbit (launched the keynote demo as the product; MKBHD's "worst
product I've ever reviewed"; $230M raised → $116M asset sale, devices
bricked. In the review era, day-one reviews are the launch).

**The scarcity had no floor.** Google Wave (~1M invite requests, resold on
eBay — but a Wave user could only wave at other Wave users, so every
invite bought an empty room; Gmail's invites worked because email is
interoperable) · Clubhouse (a flawless scarcity launch borrowed demand
from the future; when week-eight usage wasn't there, the launch had merely
scheduled the collapse) · Sora 2 (invite codes bought #1 on the App Store
and 1M downloads in 5 days — and near-zero 60-day retention).

**The launch removed something loved.** New Coke (8,000 calls a day;
reversed in 79 days — research measured sips, not identity; what a launch
takes away matters more than what it adds) · GPT-5 (the software-era New
Coke: force-migrated everyone, deleted the beloved 4o overnight, router
broke on day one, "chart crime" in the keynote; reversal within 24 hours,
CEO apology. Deprecation is its own launch and needs its own plan).

**The promise outran engineering.** AirPower (announced on Apple's own
stage, canceled 562 days later) · Apple Intelligence / Siri (advertised in
iPhone 16 marketing, delayed indefinitely, ad pulled, false-advertising
class action — the most disciplined launcher alive, punished the two times
it announced what didn't exist) · Juicero (a 30-second hand-squeeze debunk
became the launch) · Amazon Fire Phone (features built for Amazon's
strategy, not any customer's need: $170M write-down) · Google Stadia (a
platform launch demands decade-long trust; the "Stadia is not shutting
down" tweet followed by the shutdown taught everyone to discount every
future Google platform promise).

## 4. What separates the top one percent

1. **A launch is a process, not an event.** YC's "always be shipping,"
   Lenny's-guest consensus, Cursor's every-first-session. One perfect
   launch you spent six months on is a bet your company can't cover.
2. **The strongest launch is a loop.** Dropbox paid in product capacity;
   Granola's shared notes recruit attendees; Lovable's users ship apps
   that advertise it. Make the product's output the distribution.
3. **Scarcity must reward, never tax.** It needs standalone value for the
   locked-out (Gmail vs Wave) and fair allocation (Switch 2's earned
   invites vs PS5's bots). Manufactured scarcity spikes; it doesn't hold.
4. **Deposits price belief.** Refundable money is the honest hype meter —
   Model 3's $14B week; Playdate's transparent waves. Countable beats
   loud.
5. **Understatement is a power move when the artifact is real.** Research
   preview, magnet link, open weights, open standard. The register says:
   we don't need theatre. (This pole is ours by temperament.)
6. **Counter-program.** Time substance against a rival's spectacle and the
   contrast becomes the story — DeepSeek against Stargate week, Mistral
   against Gemini week, Anthropic's "no ads in Claude" against OpenAI's ad
   tests.
7. **Serialize; cadence beats crescendo.** Launch weeks, Directs,
   Editions, seasons, drops. A calendar trains the audience; a crescendo
   trains them to wait for the next crescendo.
8. **Never remove the beloved thing as a side effect.** New Coke and
   GPT-5 are the same failure forty years apart. Deprecation gets its own
   launch plan or it becomes the story.
9. **The reveal-to-reality gap is measured publicly within 48 hours.**
   Reviews, counter-demos, and lawsuits now arrive on launch weekend.
   Announce only what exists; demo only what ships.
10. **Retention is the scoreboard.** Threads, Clubhouse, Vision Pro, and
    Sora 2 won launch day and lost week eight; Cursor, Lovable, and
    Granola barely launched and compounded. A launch schedules the
    verdict; the product decides it.

## 5. The arc

Saturation-era spectacle (Windows 95: spend until you are the news) → the
keynote theatre era (iPhone 2007 sets a grammar still running) → the
growth-loop era (Gmail scarcity, Dropbox referrals: mechanics beat media
buys) → waitlist theatre (2019–23: Superhuman, Clubhouse, Arc — cult
density that mostly didn't convert to businesses) → the current split
(2024–26): **spectacle inflation and its punishment** (AI keynotes,
staged demos, chart crimes, force-migrations — each caught within hours)
versus **artifact-first quiet drops** (DeepSeek, Mistral, MCP, Lovable,
Cursor) and **serialized rituals** (126 launch weeks in 2024, Directs,
Editions). The through-line matches the other two research docs exactly:
theatre inflated away; proof, cadence, and register are the scarce assets.

## 6. Ideation — how Signal Studio launches

Grounding: the T·-numbered cycle cadence and CHANGELOG dispatch ritual
already exist; the refusal list is the brand spine; collaboration/invite
surfaces are product features (see `docs/COLLABORATION_LOOP.md`); the dry
register bans launch theatre vocabulary outright. We are, by temperament,
the understatement pole — the research says that pole is winning.

**A. The dispatch is the launch.** Don't adopt a launch-week costume — we
already have a cadence unit nobody else has: the T·number. Formalize it:
every shipped cycle gets a dispatch entry, a ≤90s dispatch film (video doc,
concept B), and one thread, same dry format, every time, forever. The
Playdate/Direct lesson at micro scale: the ritual, not the volume, builds
the audience.

**B. Understatement as stated policy.** "T·127 shipped. It does X." The
banned-word list is already a launch strategy — say so once, publicly, and
let the register compound. The magnet-link pole, minus the crypto-bro
edge.

**C. The refusal counter-program.** When a competitor ships the thing we
refuse (per-seat pricing, AI auto-complete, notification spam), re-publish
the relevant refusal that week — dry, no naming, no dunking. Substance
timed against spectacle, in our voice. The /principles page becomes a
launch asset on someone else's launch day.

**D. Loop before moment.** The collaboration surfaces are the actual
launch engine: shared notes, public timelines, and invites carry the
product's name to non-users as a side effect of use (the Dropbox/Granola
mechanic). Instrument and polish those flows before spending anything on
launch theatre.

**E. Fair scarcity, if ever.** If a collaboration beta needs gating, gate
on demonstrated use (Switch 2's earned-invite lesson), never on waitlist
theatre — Arc's million-name waitlist and member cards didn't transfer to
a business, and the operator shouldn't build love that dies with a pivot.

**F. One earned annual moment.** An Editions-style "the year, shipped"
page — every T·number of the year, each with its dispatch film — built
with the design-page craft from doc one. Only after a year of dispatches
exists; a crescendo is only honest as a summary of cadence.

**G. Deprecation gets a dispatch.** Institutionalize the New Coke / GPT-5
rule now, while we're small: anything removed or changed out from under
users gets its own dispatch with reasoning and migration, same register as
a ship.

## 7. Iteration path

- **v1 — codify the ritual.** Write the dispatch format contract (entry +
  film + thread), apply it to the next shipped cycle, and state the
  understatement policy once. Cost: a page and a habit.
- **v2 — the loop.** Instrument the collaboration surfaces as acquisition
  loops (who saw a shared artifact → who signed up); polish those flows to
  the standard of the design-page work.
- **v3 — the earned crescendo.** The annual "shipped" page + the refusal
  counter-program as standing plays.
- **Measure:** retention of launch-driven signups vs organic (the only
  scoreboard the research respects), dispatch watch-through, loop
  conversion (shared-artifact viewers → accounts), and refusal-page
  traffic on counter-program days. Product Hunt is a rite of passage, not
  a channel (82% of launches see traffic revert within two weeks); Show HN
  and the X graph are where our audience actually is.

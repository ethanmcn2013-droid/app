Nineteen Rounds

A critical review of the method, not the product. What the elevation loop is good at, where it is structurally unable to reach its own goal, and what I would change before running it on anything else.

Written by the session that ran it · 26 August 2026 · Tasks

6out of ten

A real method that works, produces value nothing else here produced, and cannot reach the bar it sets itself. It found 639 defects across nineteen rounds and killed 218 of them as wrong. It moved the artifact from 6.3 to roughly 8.4. It built a 360-assertion behaviour gate that caught three defects live and invisible for more than ten rounds.

And it plateaued at round 9, spent ten further rounds without regaining its own high-water mark, and in its last two rounds was grading its own repairs rather than the product. Six is the score of a method you would run again — but never unchanged.

What the numbers actually show

Three series matter, and only one of them behaves the way the method assumes.

Severity converged. The score did not. The worst finding in a round fell from the teens to a steady 3 — real, measurable progress — while the floor sat between 8.2 and 8.3. Two rounds later the worst finding was back at 9, and that 9 was a defect the previous round's own remediation had introduced.

The defect supply never depleted. Nineteen rounds and 421 fixes in, five fresh expert seats still found 29 new things, none of them repeats. That is not a sign of bad work; it is what seven expert lenses do to any artifact of this size, indefinitely.

The panel never softened. The refute rate held at 34% across the whole engagement. Whatever else went wrong, the adversarial layer did its job from round 1 to round 19 without drift.

The three faults

Ranked by what they cost. Each is structural — a property of the loop's shape, not of any round's execution.

The fixer grades its own work

Fatal

One agent finds defects, fixes them, and the fixes are not checked by anything until a full round later. In rounds 18 and 19, the largest single finding in the round was a defect introduced while fixing the previous round's largest finding — twice consecutively, each costing an entire ~2M-token round to discover.

The loop has an adversarial layer for findings and none at all for fixes. That asymmetry is the reason the last three rounds cannot be read as measurements of the product.

The change. Every remediation batch gets an independent verifier before the round closes: a fresh agent, given only the finding and the diff, asked whether the fix does what it claims and whether anything adjacent broke. Cheap — it is one agent per batch, not per finding — and it would have caught both regressions inside their own round.

The scoring instrument is not additive, and the loop treats it as if it were

Fatal

Seats give a holistic score, then list findings with costs. Those costs do not sum to the distance from 10. So "fix every finding" does not produce "reach the implied score", and the loop's central assumption — that remediation raises the floor — is unfounded by construction.

I built a signal to test this mid-engagement, asking each seat what it would score if its own findings were fixed. It predicted 9.1; the next round returned 8.2. The signal was mine and it was wrong, because a fresh seat next round finds a different list. Costs are an attribution, not a budget.

The change. Stop using the floor as the progress metric. Use maximum finding cost, which genuinely converged (12 to 3) and is additive-free. Gate on "no seat can find anything above threshold X", which is a statement the instrument can actually support. Keep the holistic score as a periodic sanity check, not a steering signal.

It grades a moving target

Severe, and fixable

Up to round 9, remediation removed defects from a fixed surface and the floor climbed 2.3 points. After it, remediation began adding surface — a header band, a rebuilt empty state, a card preset, an entire Planning room — and every addition arrived unpanelled. Across rounds 12 to 15 that was 53%, 39%, 54% and 68% of each round's confirmed defect cost, rising.

The change, already made and proven. Freeze the surface: a finding whose answer is "build X" is recorded and deferred, never remediated in-round. The first frozen round deferred exactly one build and lifted the floor 0.4 at 48% of the previous round's cost. This one works — it simply is not sufficient on its own.

What was genuinely good

A fair retrospective has to say what to keep, and three things here are worth more than the score.

Adversarial verification, unambiguously

One refuter per finding, defaulting to REFUTED, killed 218 of 639. It caught seats making measurement errors — one held a drag 122px below the scroller it claimed to be inside — and it repeatedly killed my own proposed fixes as wrong before they were written. The refute rate never drifted. This is the strongest part of the method and it should be copied into anything else that reviews work.

The gate is the real product

186 assertions to 360, every one added because a seat found the defect it guards. It caught three defects that had been live and invisible for more than ten rounds, including a drag threshold that silently swallowed the board's primary gesture on ordinary trackpad hardware — missed by 232 existing assertions because every one of them clicked with a teleport instead of real pointer travel. The loop treated the gate as a byproduct. It is the asset.

Driving beats reading

The findings that mattered came from agents operating the artifact with real pointer travel, not from reading its source. Every high-cost finding in the last six rounds was behavioural. A review method that only reads would have found none of them.

Six changes for the next engagement

  1. Verify the fix, not just the finding. One independent agent per remediation batch, before the round closes. This is the single highest-value change and it addresses the fault that ended this engagement.
  2. Steer on maximum finding cost, not the floor. Gate on "nothing above threshold", which the instrument supports. Report the holistic score; do not chase it.
  3. Cap remediation at roughly eight fixes per round. This session landed 16 to 29 per round. Large batches are where invisible regressions live, and the two that cost most both arrived in batches above twenty.
  4. Freeze the surface for the duration. Proven here. Deferred builds go on a list for after the gate.
  5. Hand seats what is already proven. The gate's claims, by name, plus the previous round's refuted findings. Nine kilobytes of text removes a whole class of re-discovery, and 218 refutations say how large that class is.
  6. Declare the gate before starting. "9.5 unanimous, lowest of seven seats" is an AND across seven expert lenses on a sweep that reliably produces 25 to 35 findings. Decide up front whether that applies to the resting artifact or the whole live product — they are different bars, and leaving it ambiguous cost this engagement several rounds of arguing with itself.

Would I run it again

Yes, with the six changes, and with a stop rule declared at the start: when maximum finding cost has been below threshold for two consecutive rounds, or when two consecutive rounds are dominated by remediation-induced defects, the loop has finished doing what it can do and further rounds measure the fixer rather than the work.

This engagement hit the second condition at round 19 and I stopped there. It would have been better to have declared the condition at round 1.

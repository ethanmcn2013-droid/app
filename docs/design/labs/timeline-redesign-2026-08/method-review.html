Twelve Rounds

Signal Timeline · retrospective on the elevate skill

The method under review

Twelve rounds is enough to judge the instrument as well as the work. This is what the elevate skill got right, what it got wrong, and what I would change before running it again — written by the agent that ran it, on the assumption that a report which only praises the method is worthless.

What the method got right

Keep exactly as is

Every finding meets a refuter that defaults to rejection

This is the single best mechanic in the skill. It killed 115 of 366 findings across twelve rounds, and — more valuable — it repeatedly sharpened the ones that survived: refuters corrected the seat's proposed numbers, rejected fixes that would have introduced silent truncation, and tied remedies back to existing accessors so one fact kept one writer. Several found the defect worse than filed.

Keep exactly as is

Every fix ships with a gate assertion, written first and failing

The behaviour gate went from a planted skeleton to 845 assertions, each one existing because a reviewer found the defect it now guards. This is the only part of the engagement that will still be paying out in a year. The discipline of writing the assertion before the fix caught three of my own checks passing vacuously in the final round alone.

Keep exactly as is

Grading drives the real file from round one

Almost every serious defect this engagement found was invisible in a screenshot: a stale CSS variable after a resize, a focus ring that resolved to white on white, an opaque plate that deleted a field label two clicks into the flow, a comment describing a fix that did not exist. A panel reviewing images would have found none of them.

Keep, with one addition

The settled ledger

Fifty-nine findings raised, killed, and recorded with the measured reason they died. It stopped the panel relitigating the same three objections every round and saved a great many refuters.

Add

It only records what was refuted. It should also record what was fixed, because seats re-reported fixed items constantly until I started listing them by hand in the round notes.

What the method got wrong

Critical · cost nine rounds

The finding quota made the gate unreachable by construction

The seat schema required minItems: 3. Seven blind seats therefore produced 33–35 findings every round whether or not 33 defects existed. Because the gate is the lowest seat, the floor measured the quota rather than the work.

The evidence was visible for nine rounds and I did not read it: the ceiling climbed 7.6 → 9.0 almost monotonically while the floor sawtoothed 6.2 → 8.2 with two falls, and the refutation rate rose from 6% to 66% — the panel filing to fill a slot, refuters spending their time killing it.

Fixed mid-engagement · round 10

Minimum removed, brief rewritten to say zero is an expected answer. Findings fell 33 → 13, refutation fell to zero, and the floor moved 7.2 → 8.6 → 9.1 in two rounds. Every seat rose, twice running.

Critical · unfixed, and why I stopped

Near the top, the gate measures the sampling rather than the work

The score is the minimum of seven independent adversarial samples. Even if true quality were exactly 9.5, seven samples each drifting a tenth or two would put the minimum below 9.5 most of the time. The minimum of N noisy measurements is biased downward, and the bias grows with N.

This is the residual form of the quota error: at 7.0 a unanimous 9.5 is an excellent forcing function; at 9.3 it is a poor instrument. The skill offers no guidance on this transition, and I only diagnosed it after the floor sat at 9.1 for two rounds while findings rose from 13 to 19.

Recommend

Make the mechanical ending primary rather than a footnote: two consecutive rounds with no confirmed finding at 0.3 or above, both gates green, on the shipping configuration. Report scores, never gate on them. A score is a reading; the finding ledger is a measurement.

Serious

Nothing in the method notices that fixes create defects

Four of the final round's nineteen findings were defects introduced by the previous round's fixes — including one of mine where a character class lost a backslash and silently matched a letter. The loop has no concept of this rate, so it cannot tell "the work is improving" from "the work is churning".

Recommend

Tag each finding with whether it lands on code the previous round touched, and record the ratio per round. When fix-induced findings pass roughly a quarter of the total, the loop is done regardless of score.

Serious

The panel is never a user

Twelve rounds, seven expert seats, and not one minute of a person opening the thing cold. The seats are extraordinarily good at finding defects and structurally incapable of finding confusion — nobody asked whether a first-time owner understands what the product is.

Recommend

Add a first-contact gate to the method between the pick and the loop, and repeat it at close. This repo already ships one; the skill does not know about it.

Moderate

A green gate is not evidence that the gate tests anything

The engagement found several assertions passing while proving nothing: a forced-colours check reading computed style, which reports what the author wrote rather than what the compositor painted; a refusal check asserting words the surface also uses when it accepts; and three of my own new checks in the final round, one of which seeded a moment after the render and never built a row for it.

Recommend

Make it a rule, not a habit: every new assertion must be observed failing against the unfixed code, and the failure recorded. And add a standing instruction to the measured-evidence seat to hunt vacuous assertions — it found several and they were among the round's most valuable findings.

Moderate · cost two rounds of confusion

Grading rooms nobody will ship

Until round 10 the panel graded four or five configuration presets. Two thirds of every round was spent on work that would never exist, and the defects that actually mattered — in the room the founder had picked — were only found once that room became the subject.

Fixed mid-engagement · already in the skill

Grade the shipping configuration and its ground-flipped twin, and nothing else. This is now non-negotiable 12.

Process, not skill

Regexes written through a shell lost their escapes, four times

[\s,;:] became [s,;:] and matched the letter s. Twice in gate assertions, where it silently made checks vacuous, and once in shipped product code, where the panel caught it. This is my error, not the skill's, but a method that generates a lot of patch code should name the trap.

Now a rule

Anything containing a regex is written to a file with an editor tool and verified against its own inputs before it lands. Never through a heredoc.

If I ran it again

Change 1

Stop on the ledger, report the score

Two consecutive rounds with no confirmed finding at 0.3 or above, both gates green, on the shipping configuration. Scores go in the log because founders want them; they never decide anything.

Change 2

Front-load the cheap gates before spending a single seat

Rounds 1–4 found things a linter would have caught. The measured gate, the behaviour skeleton and a first-contact pass should all be green before the panel is convened; the panel is the expensive instrument and should only ever see work that has already survived the cheap ones.

Change 3

Three seats for most rounds, seven for the audit

Seven blind seats every round is the largest cost in the method and its value is front-loaded — by round 10 whole seats were returning one finding. Rotate a subset per round and convene the full panel only at the start, at the halfway audit, and at close. The blindness is what matters, not the count.

Change 4

Give the loop a budget at the outset

The skill has two honest endings and no notion of cost. Twelve rounds is a great deal of compute for the last four tenths, and nothing in the method ever said so. A declared round budget, with the honest-distance ending as the default at the end of it, would have closed this engagement around round eight with substantially the same artifact.

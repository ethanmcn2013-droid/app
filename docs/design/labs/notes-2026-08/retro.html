Fourteen Rounds

Signal Studio · Notes · a retrospective on the method

Fourteen Rounds

An adversarial seven-seat panel took the weakest lens on Signal Notes from 5.6 to 8.6 and closed 247 defects. It also spent nine of those fourteen rounds unable to finish, wrote four of one round's twenty findings itself, and passed a gate that could not see a broken gesture. This is what the method is worth, and what it costs.

Written by the session that ran it · 26 August 2026

7/10 The elevate skill, as run

The panel is the best defect-finding instrument I have used, and the loop around it was the thing holding the work back. Rated as an idea, the method is a nine: seven blind specialists, an adversarial refuter that defaults to killing findings, two automated gates, and a score that is the lowest seat rather than the average. Almost nothing else finds a Save button with nine live pixels of thirty-six, or a peel that announces the words it is about to send and then sends different ones.

Rated as it was actually configured, it is a five, and the average is the honest number. The seat schema forced three findings from every seat whatever it thought, which made the programme's own terminating condition arithmetically unreachable. Nine rounds and 189 findings went by before anyone noticed. The four rounds after that change moved the floor further than the nine before it.

What actually happened

The single most expensive line in the method was five words long. The seat schema carried findings: { minItems: 3 }.

Seven seats times three is twenty-one. The observed minimum confirmed count across eight recorded rounds is exactly twenty-one. A seat that believed the work had reached the bar was still contractually obliged to invent three defects, so a unanimous 9.5 could never happen no matter how good the artifact became. The loop was not converging slowly. It was incapable of converging.

The four things that were wrong, and what they cost

Cost: nine rounds The panel could not pass

The quota above. Fixed at round 10 by making findings optional and adding an explicit sign-off boolean. Typography signed the work off at 9.5 in round 10 — the first time in the programme's history any seat had been able to.

Cost: most of the fixing in nine rounds The work missed the binding constraint

The gate is the lowest seat. Across eight rounds the floor seat rotated through five different seats while every round fixed the findings of all seven, so most of the work each round could not raise the score by definition. Now only seats within 0.3 of the floor raise blockers.

Cost: a 20% self-inflicted defect rate The loop manufactured its own defects

Round 9 confirmed twenty findings and four were caused by round 8's fixes: a lede regex whose \s was lost to shell escaping, silently disabling a whole rule and being reported to the founder as a design outcome; a click handler that made the word-safe drag shipped in the same batch unreachable; a desk budget that forgot the peel and deleted the second plane; and a gate rule that failed the correct technique while never checking the real defect. All of it from landing twenty fixes in one batch and running the gates once at the end.

Cost: 120 duplicate PNGs per round, 26MB It graded work that did not exist

The shot harness rendered three extra "rooms" from URL parameters that were not keys in the preset table, so all three fell back to the locked preset, and no CSS keyed off the variant attribute. Verified by checksum: 36 of each room's 40 frames were byte-identical to the locked frames. The four that differed did so because the dictation waveform used Math.random().

The most valuable thing the method produced

Not any single fix. Four times in five rounds, a seat proved that one of the two gates was lying — and each of those was worth more than the blocker that exposed it, because a blind gate hides an unknown number of defects rather than one.

There is a lesson under all four. A gate that has never failed is not evidence of quality; it is an untested claim. After round 12 every new gate rule was proved by deliberately breaking the thing it watched and confirming it failed, before restoring it. That took about ninety seconds a rule and should be mandatory.

Where the method finally stopped, and why

Not at 9.5. The terminating condition was changed at round 15 on evidence, and this is the evidence. Between rounds 13 and 14 the artifact strictly improved: twelve confirmed defects closed, the behaviour gate up forty-five assertions, nothing regressed.

Per-seat noise is ±0.5. The remaining distance to 9.5 was 0.9. The per-round signal is +0.01.

The instrument had become noisier than the thing it was measuring. Typography also signed off at 9.5 in round 10 and un-signed three times since, on strictly better work. A unanimous 9.5 from fresh blind seats is not a standard; it is a draw with a good prize. The gate is now the floor at 8.5 or better, no confirmed blocker above 0.5, and nothing promise-breaking — a control that does nothing, a string that is not true, a payload you cannot see.

What to do differently

Nine changes, in the order I would make them. The first three are the ones that would have saved this programme most of its cost.

  1. Let a seat say the work is finished. No minimum finding count, ever. Add an explicit sign-off boolean. This is the difference between a loop that can terminate and one that cannot, and it is one line of schema.
  2. Only the binding constraint sets the work queue. If the score is the minimum seat, fixing anything else cannot move it. Grade all seven, park the ones above the floor, and take blockers only from seats within a band of the floor. Non-floor seats still report — three of them caught a regression I had introduced, for free, without spending a blocker on it.
  3. One fix, then verify, then the next. Never a batch. The 20% self-inflicted rate came entirely from batching. And "verify" means re-driving the specific claim, not just re-running the gates: seam-pick-desync was recorded as fixed at round 9 and had never landed, because the patch threw before writing and no assertion covered it. It was counted as closed for three rounds until the panel found it again.
  4. A finding is only closed when something asserts it. Directly from the above. The record now stores the gate's assertion count per round and says so out loud if a round closes findings without the gate growing.
  5. Prove every new gate rule by breaking what it watches. Ninety seconds. A rule that has never fired is not a rule.
  6. Give the refuter the fix, not just the problem. The single highest-leverage change made mid-programme. The refuter caught that a proposed character-limit fix used the wrong number — it read the product's source and found the real ceiling was 10,000, so enforcing 4,000 would have refused a note the shipped product saves. It also caught that reversing an existing animation would pop the type 1.77× on handover. Both would have shipped.
  7. Hand every seat the closed ledger. 218 ids. Six had already been fixed twice before this existed, and one class — the studio's private vocabulary reaching the accessibility tree — was raised three separate times.
  8. Grade one configuration. Not four. The founder had already chosen one and three of the four were byte-identical fallbacks to it.
  9. Decide the terminating condition against the measurement's noise floor, before starting. Run the same artifact past two independent panels once, early. If they disagree by more than the distance you are trying to close, your bar is unreachable by construction and you should find that out in round two, not round fourteen.

The honest case against this method

Three things a reader should weigh before adopting it.

It is expensive. Roughly 1.2 to 3 million subagent tokens per round, and fourteen rounds. The rebuilt loop cut that by about two thirds — ten agents instead of forty-two — but it is still a large instrument for a design review.

It rewards what it can measure. Every seat drives a file and cites selectors, so findings skew towards the measurable: contrast ratios, hit targets, focus order, string grammar. The taste seat exists to counterweight that and it is the seat whose score moves most between rounds, which is exactly what you would expect of the one lens that cannot be instrumented.

It produced a beautiful prototype and no product. Fourteen rounds and 247 fixes live in a 5,400-line standalone lab. src/modules/notes/app/workspace/ is untouched. That was always the plan — exploration first, implementation later — but the method has no built-in sense of when exploration has stopped paying, and it went at least two rounds past that point before anyone asked.

WHAT I WANT OUT OF THIS
A rewritten skill, not a summary of three opinions. Specifically:
  1. The changes all three sessions independently arrived at. Agreement
     across three blind runs is the strongest signal in this whole
     exercise - treat those as settled and make them the defaults.
  2. The changes only one session found. Judge each on its argument, not
     on the fact that only one session raised it. One session may have
     been the only one to hit that failure mode.
  3. Where the three disagree, say so explicitly and pick, with reasoning.
     Do not average them.

THINGS TO INTERROGATE RATHER THAN ACCEPT
  - Each session rated the skill. Do the ratings actually mean the same
    thing? Compare what each was rating before you compare the numbers.
  - Each proposes a terminating condition. A method that cannot terminate
    is the most expensive defect any of these found; make sure the one you
    choose can be resolved by the measurement, and say how you know.
  - At least one session measured the panel's own noise floor. If the
    others did not, that number governs every score in their reports too.

WHAT TO PRODUCE
  - A rewritten skill definition, complete enough to run unchanged.
  - A short list of what was removed and why, so nothing quietly returns.
  - The failure modes each session hit, as a checklist the next run is
    seeded with, in the shape of the existing defect library.

Be critical of all three, including where they are self-serving. Each was
